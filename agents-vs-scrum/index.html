A small case study: extra coordination and review may add cost without a shown benefit.

One owner, one product, the same 30 small tasks done twice: once by agents briefed directly, once through a scrum-style queue with a planner and a reviewer.

By Guy Eden

What we wanted to find out

When AI agents write your software, should you copy a human scrum team: a planner, an architect, workers and an independent reviewer passing work along a queue? Or should one lead session brief plain agents and merge their work?

Our bet was that structure buys quality: a reviewer who never saw the code should catch what its author misses, at some cost. The scrum-style queue ran for about two days and was then switched off. So we replayed the same 30 small tasks both ways and compared what each cost and how good the result was.

The lead evidence: the 30-task pilot

The same 30 items, the same written spec and the same starting commit for each item, on Sonnet 5.5 on both sides.

$0.51per item, scrum-style queueharness figure; includes the workers and the reviewer's share
~$0.28per item, direct agentsestimate at list prices

Read these two numbers with their caveats. The direct lead session's briefing, review and merging costs are not included. The scrum planner's cost is not included. The two figures were computed by different methods, a harness figure against a list-price estimate. As an observation, the reviewer accounted for most of the scrum-style cost: 13 reviews cost $8.93, against $6.36 for all 30 workers. The direct workers used more tokens than the queue workers; the internal records disagree on the queue's total, so no number is given.

Quality: no clear quality advantage detected

Each side's changes were graded blind, without the grader knowing which side they came from, by one reviewer model (three agents, ten items each).

23 of 30direct items met acceptance
23 of 30scrum-style items met acceptance
1 of 30direct items had a high-severity issue
1 of 30scrum-style items had a high-severity issue

Thirty items cannot tell a real difference from noise, and one reviewer model did all the grading. The result is that no clear quality advantage was detected, not a finding that the two are alike.

Every role and the model it ran on

Direct

Work goes in the queue, an agent takes an item, the main session integrates: it briefs, reads each diff, rebases, runs the ratchet, merges (fast-forward) and runs the merge check.

Scrum

Personas claim items; scripts do the waiting. Retired after two days.

Both shapes consume the same queue of items; it is drawn once in each so the diagrams stay apart. Shape says the kind of role: circle the human, hexagon a script, shield the reviewer, blob an agent, rounded diamond the main session. Colour says the model (blue Sonnet, purple Fable, grey no model, not recorded, or mixed). A dashed outline marked sometimes is a step that runs only for some items. The direct agents' model varied by day (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku); the nightly review, on Fable, is not drawn. What this does and does not show

One owner, one product, small independent tasks of 20 to 75 changed lines, one run each. On those tasks the scrum-style queue cost more per item, by the figures above and with their caveats, and no clear quality advantage was detected. That suggests extra coordination and review may add cost without a demonstrated benefit on small independent tasks.

It shows nothing about large or entangled work, other models, other teams, or agent workflows in general. Leaving out the main session's own cost on the direct side matters: the direct figure is a lower bound.

Not a controlled experiment beyond the 30-task pilot, and the pilot is one run of each side. Data: the harness's token ledger, the work queue's records and the pilot's blind grading, from late October.
