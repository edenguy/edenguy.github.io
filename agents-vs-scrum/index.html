Two weeks of records: neither way was clearly cheaper or faster per item.

One owner, one product, eleven days of agent work: three days through a scrum-style queue with a planner and a reviewer, eight days of agents briefed directly by one lead session. Items per day, time per item, price per item, from the records, side by side.

By Guy Eden

What we wanted to find out

When AI agents write your software, should you copy a human scrum team: a planner, an architect, workers and an independent reviewer passing work along a queue? Or should one lead session brief plain agents and merge their work?

Our bet was that structure buys quality: a reviewer who never saw the code should catch what its author misses, at some cost. The queue ran for three days and was switched off as too slow and expensive; the direct agents ran on alone from then. This page puts the two periods' records side by side, deduplicated, with each number's definition next to it, and a 30-task replay of the same items as a second check.

The lead evidence: two weeks of records

Scrum-style queue: 21 to 23 October, 309 merged items. Direct agents: 24 to 31 October, 732 agents briefed, 286 of them landed at least one commit on main. Different tasks, different days; the samples are large enough to average, and the caveats are in one block below.

The queue's first day (74 items) ran its personas inside the session with one reused reviewer, which the queue fixed the next day; those agents price at list to about $8.60 an item, and counting them the queue comes to $3.16 an item over all 309. The two-day figure above is the queue as it was designed to run.

On these records the queue merged more items a day; its items were small, planner-specced tasks. Per item, per commit and per minute of work the two came out within a factor of two of each other, in different directions on different measures. No clear advantage for either way.

What could confound this
  • Different tasks: the queue did small items a planner cut from the backlog (most sized small); the direct agents did whatever the lead session handed out, from cleanup sweeps of forty commits to one-line fixes.
  • Different models: the queue's workers were Sonnet 5.5, its reviewer Fable 5.1; the direct agents were Sonnet 5.5 most days, Opus 5.5 on 25 October (81 of 93 agents), Haiku 5.5 for a type-annotation sweep. The Opus and Haiku agents are in the item and token counts but not in the dollars.
  • Both ran together on 21 to 23 October, on one laptop with the owner's own builds; there is no queue-only day. The direct-only days are 24 to 31 October, so the direct figures come from those.
  • The lead session's briefing, diff reading and merging are not in the direct cost; it also committed 1,041 times itself in those eight days. The queue's planner and reviewer are in its cost.
  • A merged queue item and a landed brief are not the same unit. 74 of the queue's 205 long-run items were reclaimed after a restart and paid a fresh worker's orientation; 446 of the 732 direct agents landed nothing on main (reviewers, verifiers, explorers, dropped branches).
  • The harness's own cost runs 4 to 23 percent above the list table, so the queue's dollars sit slightly higher on the same tokens than the direct side's would.

Second check: the 30-task replay

The same 30 small items, the same written spec and the same starting commit for each, on Sonnet 5.5 on both sides. A small sample; it is here as a like-for-like check, not as the lead.

$0.51per item, scrum-style queueharness figure; workers and the reviewer's share, planner not included
~$0.28per item, direct agentsestimate at list prices; the lead session not included

In the replay the queue cost about twice as much per item, and the difference was the reviewer: 13 reviews cost $8.93 against $6.36 for all 30 workers. The two-week record above does not repeat that gap: there the queue's per-item price is the lower of the two. A 30-item replay and an eleven-day record disagree, and this page shows both rather than pick one.

Quality: no clear quality advantage detected

Each side's 30 changes were graded blind, without the grader knowing which side they came from, by one reviewer model (three agents, ten items each).

23 of 30direct items met acceptance
23 of 30scrum-style items met acceptance
1 of 30direct items had a high-severity issue
1 of 30scrum-style items had a high-severity issue

Thirty items cannot tell a real difference from noise, and one reviewer model did all the grading. No clear quality advantage was detected; that is not a finding that the two are alike. The two-week periods were not reviewed in a comparable way, so they add no quality figure.

Every role and the model it ran on

Direct

Work goes in the queue, an agent takes an item, the main session integrates: it briefs, reads each diff, rebases, runs the ratchet, merges (fast-forward) and runs the merge check.

Scrum

Personas claim items; scripts do the waiting. Retired after two days.

Both shapes consume the same queue of items; it is drawn once in each so the diagrams stay apart. Shape says the kind of role: circle the human, hexagon a script, shield the reviewer, blob an agent, rounded diamond the main session. Colour says the model (blue Sonnet, purple Fable, grey no model, not recorded, or mixed). A dashed outline marked sometimes is a step that runs only for some items. The direct agents' model varied by day (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku); the nightly review, on Fable, is not drawn. Models are the ones recorded in the agent definitions and the token ledger; where none is recorded, the block says so.

What this does and does not show

One owner, one product, eleven days, about a thousand agent runs and three hundred merged queue items, with no like-for-like control between the two periods. On this record neither way was clearly cheaper or faster per item: the queue merged more items a day and spent fewer tokens per commit; the direct agents cost about the same per commit, more per item at the mean and less at the median, and worked an item in about the same minutes. The one measurement with no counterpart is the queue's two-hour median wait from queued to merged, which the direct side does not record. The 30-task replay found the queue twice the price per item; the larger record did not.

It shows nothing about large or entangled work, other models, other teams, or agent workflows in general. Leaving out the lead session's own cost on the direct side matters: every direct figure is a lower bound. Where the queue was switched off as too slow and expensive, these records support "slow to merge an item" and do not support "more expensive per item".

Not a controlled experiment beyond the 30-task replay, and the replay is one run of each side. Data: the harness's token ledger (deduplicated to one line per agent; a long-lived agent's earlier lines re-list its commits), the queue's item files and spawn ledger, git history, and the replay's blind grading, 21 to 31 October.
