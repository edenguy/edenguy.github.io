Agents that just work beat a scrum team of agents.

Same product, same owner, two ways to run AI coding agents for a week. What each one cost, and what it shipped.

By Guy Eden

What we wanted to prove

When AI agents write your software, should you copy a human scrum team: a planner, an architect, workers and an independent reviewer passing work along a queue? Or should one lead session brief plain agents and merge their work?

Our bet was that structure buys quality. A reviewer who never saw the code should catch what its author misses, at some cost in speed. So we ran both on the same product and counted three things: how fast work lands, how many tokens it burns, and how many critical or high bugs slip through.

The controlled subset

The same 30 small, independent items, done twice: once by agents briefed directly, once through the queue. Two reviewers graded every change blind, without knowing which side it came from.

One high-severity bug on each side, so quality did not separate them. Thirty items cannot tell a real difference from noise. The direct figures leave out the main session's briefing, and the scrum figures leave out its planner.

Same quality, about twice the speed, a sixth of the tokens. A queue earns its keep only on unattended runs and on work that shares files.

Not a controlled experiment: the two modes ran in different weeks on different tasks. Tokens are fresh tokens plus cache writes at 1.25 and cache reads at 0.1. A 30-item pilot of identical work found the same shape: about 50 items an hour against 20, and one high-severity bug on each side. Data: the harness's token ledger and the code-review records of one product, 21 to 27 October.
